## Highlights
- Flies possess "reward" neurons with properties similar to those of the vertebrate MFB
- Closed-loop stimulation of these neurons enables flies to learn a spatial task
- Flies' learning is influenced both by vision and non-visual features in the arena
- Learning is enabled by specific DA and ChAT neurons as well as MB and CX in the brain

## Summary
The ability to use memory to return to specific locations for foraging is advantageous for survival. Although recent reports have demonstrated that the fruit flies Drosophila melanogaster are capable of visual cue-driven place learning and idiothetic path integration [1–4], the depth and flexibility of Drosophila's ability to solve spatial tasks and the underlying neural substrate and genetic basis have not been extensively explored. Here, we show that Drosophila can remember a reward-baited location through reinforcement learning and do so quickly and without requiring vision. After gaining genetic access to neurons (through 0273-GAL4) with properties reminiscent of the vertebrate medial forebrain bundle (MFB) and developing a high-throughput closed-loop stimulation system, we found that both sighted and blind flies can learn—by trial and error—to repeatedly return to an unmarked location (in a rectangularly shaped arena) where a brief stimulation of the 0273-GAL4 neurons was available for each visit. We found that optogenetic stimulation of these neurons enabled learning by employing both a cholinergic pathway that promoted self-stimulation and a dopaminergic pathway that likely promoted association of relevant cues with reward. Lastly, inhibiting activities of specific neurons time-locked with stimulation of 0273-GAL4 neurons showed that mushroom bodies (MB) and central complex (CX) both play a role in promoting learning of our task. Our work uncovered new depth in flies' ability to learn a spatial task and established an assay with a level of throughput that permits a systematic genetic interrogation of flies' ability to learn spatial tasks.

## Results and Discussion

### Flies Possess Neurons with Properties Similar to Those of the Vertebrate Medial Forebrain Bundle (MFB)
We set out to investigate flies' capacity for using memory to return to a rewarded "goal location." We first looked for a reward that is more powerful than natural rewards and can be controlled with spatial and temporal precision. We wondered whether flies possess neurons similar to those in the vertebrate MFB: neurons whose direct stimulation is so "attractive" that animals would vigorously seek their stimulation at the expense of pressing needs [5]. We screened many candidate GAL4 lines and found one (0273-GAL4) that, when used to express the red-shifted channelrhodopsin CsChrimson [6], caused flies to become strongly attracted to red light (Figures 1A–1C and S1A–S1C). 0273-GAL4 has been shown to label the PAM cluster of dopaminergic (DA) neurons that mediates rewards during classical odor learning [7–9]. We found that light seeking of flies that expressed CsChrimson in their 0273-GAL4 neurons ("0273>CsC" flies) was robust at the level of individuals, lasted for hours, did not require vision, and was substantially stronger than their seeking natural rewards; for example, starved males preferred light over sucrose and egg-laying females preferred light over acetic acid (AA), an otherwise robustly preferred egg-laying cue [10, 11] (Figures 1D–1F, 1H, 1I, S1D, and S1E; Video S1). Further, flies' seeking optogenetic stimulation of their 0273-GAL4 neurons was substantially stronger than their seeking optogenetic stimulation of several groups of neurons that are known to mediate sugar reward or drive self-stimulation [9, 12, 13] (Figure 1G). Lastly, flies still strongly pursued light, even if it simultaneously stimulated both the 0273-GAL4 neurons and the avoidance-promoting bitter-sensing taste neurons [14, 15] (Figure 1G).

### A Learning Task that Uses Optogenetic Stimulation of 0273-GAL4 Neurons as Reward
We next used SkinnerTrax [15]—a system we developed for closed-loop light delivery to multiple animals in parallel (Figure 2A; Video S2)—to implement a learning task where stimulation of the 0273-GAL4 neurons was the reward. Our learning task consisted of two foraging sessions and pre- and post-session periods, and light was available only during the foraging sessions (Figure 2B). To obtain one (250 ms) pulse of light, flies needed to enter a software-defined circle (i.e., cross its "border" from the outside), although staying inside or exiting the circle would not trigger any pulses (Figure 2B). This "pulse upon entry only" rule allowed us to assess whether flies can return to the reward-baited circle from different positions in the arena, as termination of light tended to invigorate the flies (Video S1), causing them to disperse from the rewarded circle.

Our task has two noteworthy features. First, unlike Ofstad et al. [1], we did not provide specific visual cues to signal the position of the reward-baited circle. But the arena was dimly lit and had a few features (e.g., agarose strips) that could aid navigation (Figures 1A and S2I). Second, we examined learning at the level of individuals and paired each experimental fly with a yoked control with the same genotype (Figure 2B). The yoked control could not trigger rewards itself but instead received a reward "for free" exactly when the experimental fly triggered a reward (Video S3).

### Flies Can Learn the Task by Using Visual and Non-visual Cues
Several results suggest that flies were capable of learning the spatial task we implemented. First, positional heatmaps showed that the experimental flies clearly preferred the reward-baited circle during foraging sessions, but yoked controls did not (Figures 2C, S2A, and S2B; Video S3). Experimental flies' preference for the reward-baited circle was also obvious when we plotted their "reward index" (RI) over time (Figure 2D): strong preference for the rewarded circle emerged in the 10 min following the session's first reward and continued to increase. In contrast, the RI of yoked controls generally stayed around zero. Second, flies' trajectories in between two consecutive rewards became shorter and more directed as the sessions progressed (Figures 2G and 2H). Interestingly, the distributions of run lengths and turn angles in experimental flies' trajectories differed significantly from those of yoked controls (Figure S2C). Third, the RI during the first post-session period showed that the experimental flies continued to prefer the previously rewarded location, even though it no longer delivered rewards (Figures 2E and 2F). Strong preference was apparent in each post-session when the rewarded location was constant (Figure S2D).

Flies likely used both non-visual and visual cues to learn the task. First, blind (norpA) flies learned the task as well as sighted ones (Figures S2E and S2F), and they did not appear to learn by scent marking the rewarded location—naive flies did not prefer a location that had just been frequented by experienced flies (Figure S2G). Second, although blind flies learned well in "regular" arenas, their learning reduced significantly when the task was administered in "flat-floor" arenas that had neither agarose strips nor a hole in the floor (Figures S2I and S2J). Sighted flies learned less well in flat-floor arenas too, but the reduction was less significant (Figure S2K). Third, some of the blind flies even developed an avoidance of agarose—an otherwise neutral cue—when the rewarded circle was placed next to an agarose well in a larger arena, as if they had learned to use agarose contact as a turn signal (Figure S2H). Collectively, these results suggest that flies can use both visual and non-visual cues to learn our task.

### 0273-GAL4 Labels Two Distinct Groups of Neurons that Support Learning
We next investigated the nature of the reward produced by stimulating the 0273-GAL4 neurons. Because 0273-GAL4 has been shown to label the PAM cluster of DA neurons that mediates rewards during classical conditioning in flies [7–9], we first tested the role of these DA neurons. First, we confirmed that 0273-GAL4 labels many PAM-DA neurons (Figures 3A and 3B), consistent with previous reports [7, 8]. Second, we found that mutants for two dopamine receptors, DR1 and D2R, both learned less well than controls (Figures 3D and 3E), as did animals with reduced TH expression in 0273-GAL4 neurons (Figures 3F, S3A, and S3B). Third, blocking CsChrimson expression in all DA neurons as well as serotonergic neurons potentially labeled by 0273-GAL4 reduced learning too, whereas blocking CsChrimson specifically in serotonergic neurons or non-PAM DA neurons did not (Figures 3G, S3E, and S3F). Curiously, blocking CsChrimson in the subpopulation of PAM-DA neurons labeled by the R58E02 enhancer did not reduce learning (Figures S3C and S3D), although this subpopulation has been shown to be necessary and sufficient for promoting odor-reward association during classical conditioning [7–9, 16]. These results suggest learning of our task may require DA release from a PAM-DA subpopulation different from the one required for appetitive odor learning.

Because 0273-GAL4 also labels many non-DA neurons (Figure 3C), we next tested whether non-DA neurons might play a role too. We found that blocking CsChrimson expression in the cholinergic neurons labeled by 0273-GAL4 reduced learning significantly (Figures 3H and S3G). Interestingly, examining flies' behaviors in an open-loop light preference assay showed that blocking CsChrimson expression in the cholinergic but not the DA subpopulation of 0273-GAL4 neurons significantly reduced flies' light-seeking interest (Figure 3I). These results suggest that stimulating 0273-GAL4 neurons may enable learning via at least two pathways: one that contains cholinergic neurons whose activation promotes flies to seek repeat activation and another that contains PAM-DA neurons whose activation allows flies to associate the specific cues that they experience at the goal location with activation of the cholinergic pathway.

### Mushroom Bodies (MB) and Central Complex (CX) Both Contribute to Learning
Next, we aimed to identify the brain centers that are important for learning of our task. We first examined the MB—a target of PAM-DA neurons and an association center [7, 9, 14, 17, 18]. We found that ablating MB with hydroxyurea (HU) [17] reduced learning in both blind and sighted flies, but the learning reduction (in session 1) was more significant in blind ones (Figures 4A, 4B, and S4A). In addition to lesioning MB, we also inhibited MB time-locked with reward delivery by expressing the green-light-sensitive chloride channel GtACR1 [19, 20] in MB, CsChrimson in 0273-GAL4 neurons, and replacing red with green light (Figure S4C). We first assessed GtACR1's effectiveness and found that, whereas control flies showed no apparent response to green light, 0273>GtACR1 flies avoided it (Figures 4C and 4E). Additionally, we confirmed that 0273>CsC flies were strongly attracted to green light (Figures 4D and 4E). We then found that inhibiting Kenyon cells (KCs) (using R13F02-lexA) [21]—the principal neurons of MB—time-locked with reward delivery reduced learning in sighted and blind flies (Figures 4F, 4G, and S4D), and, similar to MB ablation, learning reduction was stronger for blind flies than for sighted ones during the first session. Curiously, time-locked inhibition of KCs using a different MB driver (247-lexA) [22] reduced learning only in blind flies, even though the labeling patterns of the two drivers were relatively similar (Figures 4H, 4I, and S4D). These results suggest that MB plays more significant a role in enabling learning in blind flies.

Because the CX has been shown to regulate visual-cues-driven place learning and navigation [1, 23, 24], we tested whether it may regulate learning in sighted flies. Interestingly, selectively inhibiting one specific group of ring neurons (labeled by R38G08-lexA) [25] reduced learning in sighted but not blind flies (Figures S4G–S4I). Ring neurons are projection neurons that process and relay visual information to the ellipsoid body (EB) of CX, where they then signal the "compass neurons" that represent flies' heading direction and regulate navigation [26–32]. Lastly, we found that these CX- and MB-manipulated animals showed similar light preference to controls (Figures 4J, S4B, and S4J), suggesting that their learning reduction was not caused by reduced light-seeking interest. Taken together, these results suggest MB and CX both play a role in promoting learning of our task but may have differential importance in blind and sighted flies.

In this study, we have shown that flies can learn by trial and error an unmarked location where an optogenetically delivered reward is available for each visit. Their learning was fast, robust, and could take place without vision, when learning was aided by easily detectable non-visual features. Further, MB and CX both played a role, but their contributions differed for sighted and blind flies. Our study thus provides a useful system for dissecting the genetic and circuit basis for a simple form of spatial learning. How did the flies learn the goal location in our task? Given that they are able to use visual landmarks and path integration for place learning [1, 2, 23], one speculative proposal is that they may represent different locations by the location's direction and distance relative to major landmarks and store such representations in CX and/or MB. Below, we list a few challenges and opportunities for future studies and testing this proposal. First, although some of the 0273-GAL4 neurons projected to CX, the projection was weak and targeted to the fan-shaped body (FB), not EB (Figure S4K). Second, rut and dunce, two genes that are known to act in MB to promote odor learning [33–37], were unnecessary for learning of our task (Figures S4E and S4F). Third, although PAM-DA neurons were important for learning of our task and project to MB [7, 9, 14], the PAM-DA subset known to promote odor learning appeared unimportant. Further, unlike for animals where activation of DA neurons drives both reward seeking and cue-reward association [38], for flies, these two reward properties may be separately regulated by specific cholinergic and DA neurons. Lastly, although as a group flies clearly used spatial strategies for learning, some individuals might have solved the task entirely by using spatially agnostic strategies (e.g., repeating specific movements). Further, their strategies for sessions and post-sessions may differ; thus, RIs in these two periods may not always be correlated.

## STAR Methods (condensed)
- Flies: male flies except egg-laying assays; 0273-GAL4 (gift of S. Waddell) driving UAS-CsChrimson; retinal food 7–10 days in dark; circadian entrained; tasks started ~10 am. Key drivers: R58E02-GAL4/GAL80, TH-GAL80, Cha-LexA, TRH-LexA, DDC2-LexA, R38G08-LexA (ring neurons), R13F02-LexA and 247-LexA (KCs), UAS/LexAop-GtACR1, norpA, DR1 and D2R mutants, rut, dnc, UAS-TH-RNAi.
- Light attraction assay: arenas with red 624 nm LEDs beneath (typically 7% intensity, 3 μW/mm²); sucrose agarose on both sides; ~2.5 h experiments; Ctrax tracking.
- SkinnerTrax closed-loop system: Python/OpenCV tracker, 16 cameras at 320x240, 7.5 fps; TLC59711 LED driver; rewards were 250 ms pulses at 25% intensity (11 μW/mm² red, 9.6 μW/mm² green); 2 LEDs per arena illuminate the whole arena; wake-up 3 s pulse before sessions. Reward triggered on entry into a software-defined circle only. Yoked controls received identical pulses passively.
- Reward index: (entries into rewarded circle − entries into control circle)/total entries, computed in 10-min "sync buckets" from first reward (sessions) or from first control-circle visit (post-sessions); buckets with fewer than 10 entries excluded; symmetric circles so RI is compared against zero. AUC of RI curves also compared between groups.
- Speed and stop fraction restricted to floor frames (8.4 px/mm); stop defined as <2 mm/s.
- Heatmaps exclude stopped frames; RDP (epsilon 3 px) used for run length and turn angle.
- Statistics: Prism 8; parametric tests (t, Welch, Welch ANOVA), two-tailed; sample sizes are numbers of flies. Datasets available on request; code on GitHub (yanglab-ctrax, SkinnerTrax, Tlc59711).
