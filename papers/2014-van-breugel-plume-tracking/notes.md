# van Breugel & Dickinson (2014): plume tracking in free flight from three reflexes

[Published article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2013.12.023) · [Full text](cell-fulltext.md) · [Supplement (Figures S1–S6, movie legends, procedures)](mmc1-supplement.pdf) · [Exact reading coverage](reading-log.md)

## What this contributes to a complete navigation account

The natural-flight policy that any wind/odor/vision circuit account must reproduce. Over 70 h of free-flight 3D tracking in a wind tunnel (0.4 m s⁻¹ wind, spatially calibrated ethanol plume verified by photo-ionization, Figure S1) show that plume tracking decomposes into three iterative, independent reflexes that stay stable across repeated encounters (Figure S4):

1. **Surge:** 190 ± 75 ms after entering the plume, flies speed up and turn upwind. Because of the delay they typically pass through the narrow plume before turning, so olfactory experience is a series of short bursts (Figure 2). Upwind heading uses visual slip: floor stripes parallel to the wind tighten the upwind heading distribution, perpendicular stripes broaden it (visual anemotaxis in Kennedy's sense).
2. **Cast:** 450 ± 165 ms after losing the plume, flies begin vertical and horizontal crosswind casts. Cast onset is triggered by plume loss, not an internal clock, and barely depends on how long the fly was in the plume (Figure 4D). Crosswind heading is also visually controlled (perpendicular stripes tighten it), and flies cast with broad body orientations rather than holding an upwind body angle (Figure 5), so heading and body orientation are decoupled.
3. **Odor-gated visual attraction:** after odor contact flies show enhanced attraction to small visual objects (a dark post), which raises the probability of reaching the source; downward casts are not deliberate object searches (Figure 6). The effect holds for both high- and low-contrast floors (Figure S5) and for a fruit-attractant plume (Figure S2).

The authors call the composition of these reflexes stigmergic: the observed trajectories emerge from iterated reflexes without a plan or memory of plume position. This is distinct from the moth model in which a homogeneous plume triggers casting sequences. Figure S6 argues that, in the constrained tunnel, cast initiation timing may be shaped by visual expansion from the walls.

## Why it matters for the circuit program

- Assigns roles: wind direction is inferred visually in flight (consistent with Currier & Nagel 2018's finding that stripe cues dominate wind in tethered flight, and with Suver 2019's antennal wind pathway being lateralised); odor timing gates surge and cast; landmark attraction is a separate channel modulated by odor.
- The stigmergic model is the null hypothesis for flight search: no vector memory is needed to explain source finding here. Dan 2024, the Maimon & Abbott 2026 goal-direction framing, and the recent olfactory vector-strategy papers must add to, not replace, it.
- Timing constants (190 ms, 450 ms) constrain which circuits can implement each reflex.

## Caveats for synthesis

- Odor encounters are inferred from the calibrated plume geometry, not measured on the fly.
- Hungry flies, ethanol or Vector960 plume, laminar flow; turbulent plumes and other states may change gains.
- Tunnel walls may influence cast timing (Figure S6).

## Remaining questions

1. How surge/cast latencies map onto the wind-compass (Okubo 2020) and airflow-vector (Ishida 2026, May 2025) circuits during flight.
2. Whether odor-gated object attraction shares the CX goal machinery used for stripe menotaxis, or is a visual-pathway gain change.
