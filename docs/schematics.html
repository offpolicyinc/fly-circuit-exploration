Panel 1The whole loop, in five stages

Everything in the central complex (CX) can be placed on one chain. Sensory cues and self-motion feed a heading estimate; heading is combined with body-relative velocity to make world-relative vectors; a stored goal is compared with heading; the signed error becomes a turn. Memory, the box that would close the loop over time, is the part with the least evidence.

Panel 2The compass: a ring attractor with a named velocity input

Heading lives as one localized bump of activity in the ~50 EPG neurons tiling the ellipsoid body (EB). Local excitation plus broad inhibition hold the bump; two mirror-image PEN populations, offset by one column, push it left or right in proportion to rotational velocity. The velocity itself arrives through a single pair of GLNO neurons in the noduli.

Panel 3Anchoring the compass to the world: a learned inhibitory map with two modulators

The bump drifts in darkness, so it is tethered to cues. Visual and wind ring neurons (ER) inhibit EPGs; plasticity carves a trough in that inhibition at the heading each cue should map to. The learning rule needs to know which EPGs are active (postsynaptic) at each ER terminal (presynaptic). Octopamine carries the "which"; dopamine carries the "when".

Panel 4Bumps are vectors: the eight-axis coordinate system

Δ7 neurons impose a sinusoidal activity profile across the 8 + 8 glomeruli of the protocerebral bridge (PB). Any bump is then a 2-D vector: phase = angle, amplitude = length. Anatomy rotates vectors (fixed projection offsets), nodulus inputs scale them, convergence in a fan-shaped-body (FB) column adds them, and a membrane-potential switch can negate them.

Panel 5Body-relative velocity → world-relative travel direction

The fly's walking (or airflow) velocity is measured in body coordinates. PFN populations carry heading-aligned bumps whose left/right amplitudes are set by the body-relative velocity components; summing them in hΔB gives the direction of travel in world coordinates, even when the fly sideslips or walks backward.

Panel 6Goal → steering → legs

A goal heading is expressed as a bump in FC2. PFL3 neurons receive the goal (in the FB) and a copy of heading shifted by roughly ±90° per hemisphere (from the PB); their left-minus-right output approximates sin(goal − heading), a signed turn command. PFL2 rises near the anti-goal and raises the gain. Descending neurons then commit to a turn through a winner-take-all pair and drive all six legs.

Where to startSuggested order for going through these

Panels 2 and 4 are the load-bearing ideas: a ring attractor as the heading state, and sinusoidal bumps as vectors. Once those are clear, Panel 5 (vector transform) and Panel 6 (goal comparison) are the same trick applied twice. Panel 3 explains how the whole thing stays registered to the world, and Panel 7 is the honest scorecard of what behavior has actually been tied to which cells. The open problems are all in the dashed boxes: the accumulator, the goal store, and the reinforcement signal.
